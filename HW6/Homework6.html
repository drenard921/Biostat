Q3 Problem 5:

A crossover study was conducted to investigate whether oat bran cereal helps to lower cholesterol levels in hypercholesterolemic males. Fourteen such individuals were randomly placed on a diet that included either oat bran or corn flakes; after two weeks, their low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels were recorded. Each man was then switched to the alternative diet. After a second two-week period, the LDL cholesterol level of each individual was again recorded. The data from this study are shown below:

a) Are the two samples paired or independent?

The two samples are paired, because each subject consumed both oat bran and corn flakes in a crossover study design. LDL levels were measured for the same individuals under both conditions, so the observations are not independent - they are matched within subjects.

b) What are the appropriate null and alternative hypotheses for a two-sided test

Let \(\mu_D\) represent the mean difference in LDL cholesterol (Corn Flakes − Oat Bran) across all subjects.

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu_D = 0\)
    There is no difference in LDL cholesterol between the two diets.

  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): \(\mu_D \ne 0\)
    There is a difference in LDL cholesterol between the two diets.

c) Conduct the test at the 0.05 level of significance. What is the p-value?

We compute the difference in LDL cholesterol for each subject:

\[ \text{Difference}_i = \text{LDL}_{\text{Corn Flakes}, i} - \text{LDL}_{\text{Oat Bran}, i} \]

Step 1: Differences

The differences (rounded to 2 significant figures for easy viewing) are:

0.77, 0.85, -0.45, -0.26, 0.30, 0.86, 0.60, 0.62, 0.31, 0.72, 0.09, 0.16, 0.41, 0.10

Note: Full-precision values were used for all subsequent calculations.

Step 2: Mean of the Differences

\[ \bar{d} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i = 0.362857 \text{ mmol/L} \]

Step 3: Standard Deviation of the Differences

\[ s_d = 0.405964 \text{ mmol/L} \]

Step 4: Standard Error (SE)

\[ SE = \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{0.405964}{\sqrt{14}} = \frac{0.405964}{3.741657} = 0.108498 \]

Step 5: t-Statistic

\[ t = \frac{\bar{d}}{SE} = \frac{0.362857}{0.108498} = 3.344 \]

Step 6: 95% Confidence Interval

We use the critical value for a two-sided 95% confidence interval with 13 degrees of freedom (n -1 degrees, n = 14):

\[ t^* = 2.1604 \]

Margin of error:

\[ ME = t^* \cdot SE = 2.1604 \cdot 0.108498 = 0.234397 \]

Confidence interval:

\[ \bar{d} \pm ME = 0.362857 \pm 0.234397 \]

\[ \text{95\% CI} = [0.128461,\ 0.597254] \text{ mmol/L} \]

Step 7: Estimating the p-Value by Hand

We can assess statistical significance in two ways: using the confidence interval or by comparing our t-statistic to a t-distribution table.

Method 1: Use the Confidence Interval

The 95% confidence interval was:

\[ [0.128461,\ 0.597254] \]

Since this interval does not include 0, it implies that the two-sided p-value is less than 0.05, and we reject the null hypothesis.

Method 2: Use a t-table to estimate the p-value

Given: \(t = 3.3444\) and \(df = 13\)

From the t-table for 13 degrees of freedom:

  • \(t = 3.012\) corresponds to two-tailed \(p = 0.01\)
  • \(t = 3.852\) corresponds to two-tailed \(p = 0.002\)

Since \(3.012 < 3.3444 < 3.852\), we estimate:

\(p\) is between \(0.01\) and \(0.002\)

The exact p-value, calculated using R, is:

\(p = 0.005278\)

This falls within our range, confirming our t-table estimate but also illustrating the limits of printed tables — they only provide rough intervals, not precise probabilities.

Q4 Problem 8:

A study was conducted to determine whether an expectant mother’s cigarette smoking has any effect on the bone mineral content of her otherwise healthy child. A sample of 77 newborns whose mothers smoked during pregnancy has mean bone mineral content x-bar1 = 0.098 g/cm and standard deviation s1 = 0.026 g/cm; a sample of 161 infants whose mothers did not smoke has mean x-bar2 = 0.095 g/cm and standard deviation s2 = 0.025 g/cm. Assume that the underlying population variances are equal.

a) Are the two samples paired or independent?

The two samples are independent, not paired. Each measurement of bone mineral content comes from a different infant, and the infants are grouped based on whether their mothers did or did not smoke during pregnancy. There is no matching or repeated measurement across the groups, so a paired design is not appropriate.

b) State the null and alternative hypotheses of the two-sided test.

Let \(\mu_1\) be the mean bone mineral content in infants whose mothers smoked, and \(\mu_2\) be the mean in infants whose mothers did not smoke.

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu_1 = \mu_2\) (No difference in bone mineral content)
  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_A\)): \(\mu_1 \ne \mu_2\) (Difference in bone mineral content)

c) Conduct the test at the 0.05 level of significance. What do you conclude?

### Problem 8, Part c: Manual Two-Sample t-Test from Summary Stats
# Given values
x1_bar <- 0.098
s1 <- 0.026
n1 <- 77

x2_bar <- 0.095
s2 <- 0.025
n2 <- 161

# Pooled standard deviation
sp_squared <- ((n1 - 1) * s1^2 + (n2 - 1) * s2^2) / (n1 + n2 - 2)
sp <- sqrt(sp_squared)

# Standard error
se <- sp * sqrt(1/n1 + 1/n2)

# t-statistic
t_stat <- (x1_bar - x2_bar) / se

# degrees of freedom
df <- n1 + n2 - 2

# two-sided p-value
p_value <- 2 * pt(-abs(t_stat), df)

# Output
list(t_statistic = t_stat, df = df, p_value = p_value)
$t_statistic
[1] 0.8549064

$df
[1] 236

$p_value
[1] 0.39347

Note: We computed the t-statistic manually using the pooled standard deviation formula in R, as raw data were not available. This two-sample t-test assumes equal variances, as specified in the problem.

Problem 8, Part c: Conclusion

There was no statistically significant difference in bone mineral content between infants whose mothers smoked and those whose mothers did not (two-sample t-test, equal variances assumed, \(t = 0.8549\), \(df = 236\), \(p = 0.3935\)). We fail to reject the null hypothesis at the \(\alpha = 0.05\) level.
