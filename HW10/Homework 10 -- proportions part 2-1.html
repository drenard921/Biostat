12.

In a study of intra-observer variability in the assessment of cervical smears, 3325 slides were screened for the presence or absence of abnormal squamous cells. Each slide was screened by a particular observer and then rescreened six months later by the same observer. The results of this study are shown below:

First Screening 2nd: present 2nd: absent Total
Present 1763 489 2252
Absent 403 670 1073
Total 2166 1159 3325

Last week I asked you if these data support the null hypothesis that here is no association between time of screening and diagnosis. This would be a test of independence, such as a chi-square test.

This week we learned about the McNemar’s Test for paired data. McNemar’s tests a somewhat different null hypothesis: that the true proportion of slides that observer 1 would call positive is the same as the true proportion of slides that observer 2 would call positive. Use McNemar’s to see if there is a significant difference in the proportion that the two observers call positive.

McNemar’s Test for Paired Data

We are analyzing paired binary data where the same slides were assessed twice by the same observer. Our null hypothesis is:

\[ H_0: p_b = p_c \]

where
  • \(p_b\) = probability a slide is classified as present in the first screening but absent in the second,
  • \(p_c\) = probability a slide is classified as absent in the first screening but present in the second.

The McNemar test focuses only on these discordant pairs, because the slides that were classified the same way both times (the diagonal cells of the 2×2 table) do not provide evidence about disagreement.

From the data:

2nd: present 2nd: absent
1st: present 1763 489
1st: absent 403 670

We have: - \(b = 489\) (present → absent), - \(c = 403\) (absent → present).

Test Statistic

For large samples, the McNemar statistic with continuity correction is:

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c} \]

Substituting ( b = 489 ) and ( c = 403 ):

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{(|489 - 403| - 1)^2}{489 + 403} = \frac{(86 - 1)^2}{892} = \frac{85^2}{892} = 8.10 \]

This follows a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom.

P-value

\[ p = 1 - P(\chi^2_{(1)} \le 8.10) \approx 0.0044 \]

Conclusion

Since \(p < 0.05\) , we reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that the proportion of slides classified as positive differs between the first and second screening.

R Code to check work

b <- 489
c <- 403
chi_sq <- (abs(b - c) - 1)^2 / (b + c)
p_value <- 1 - pchisq(chi_sq, df = 1)

[1] 8.099776
p_value
[1] 0.004427073

15.

In an effort to evaluate the health effects of air pollutants containing sulphur, individuals living near a pulp mill in Finland were questioned about various symptoms following a strong emissions released in September 1987. The same subjects were questioned again 4 months later, during a low-exposure period. A summary of the responses related to the occurrence of headaches is presented below.

Low Exposure High Exposure Yes High Exposure No Total
Yes 2 2 4
No 8 33 41
Total 10 35 45
a. Test the null hypothesis that there is no association between exposure to air pollutants containing sulphur and the occurrence of headaches.

Since the same subjects were questioned during high exposure and again during low exposure, this is paired binary data.
To test whether the proportion of people reporting headaches differs between exposure levels, we use McNemar’s Test.

Data

For McNemar’s test, we focus on the discordant pairs: - \(b = 2\): Headache during high exposure but not during low exposure. - \(c = 8\): Headache during low exposure but not during high exposure.

Hypotheses

\[ H_0: p_b = p_c \]

The null hypothesis states that the probability of reporting a headache is the same during high exposure as during low exposure.

Test Statistic

For large samples, McNemar’s statistic with continuity correction is:

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c}. \]

Substituting \(b = 2\) and \(c = 8\):

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{(|2 - 8| - 1)^2}{2 + 8} = \frac{(6 - 1)^2}{10} = \frac{5^2}{10} = 2.5. \]

P-value

With \(df = 1\):

\[ p = 1 - P(\chi^2_{(1)} \le 2.5) \approx 0.114. \]

b. What do you conclude?

The McNemar’s test yielded \(\chi^2 = 2.5\) with \(p \approx 0.114\).
Since \(p > 0.05\), we fail to reject \(H_0\).

Interpretation: There is insufficient evidence to conclude that the probability of reporting headaches differs between the high- and low-exposure periods for the same subjects.

c. If you found a significant association, what limitations of this study would affect your ability to conclude that pollution caused headaches?

Even if we had found a significant association between sulphur pollution exposure and headaches, the study design has several limitations that would affect our ability to conclude that pollution caused the headaches:

  1. Observational Study Design:
    There was no random assignment of exposure levels. Without randomization, other factors (e.g., stress, seasonal differences, or concurrent illnesses) could confound the results.

  2. Self-Reported Symptoms:
    Headache data are based on self-reports, which can be subjective and prone to recall bias.

  3. Temporal Changes:
    The second survey was conducted four months later during a different season, and seasonal factors (such as weather or allergens) might influence headaches.

  4. No Control Group:
    The same subjects were measured twice, but there was no group of individuals unexposed to sulphur pollution over the same period to serve as a control.

  5. Small Sample Size:
    With only 45 subjects, random variation and sampling error are large, reducing the reliability of the observed association.

Conclusion: Even with a statistically significant result, these limitations mean we could not infer a causal relationship between pollution exposure and headaches.

b. Estimate the odds ratio of suffering ectopic pregnancy for women who had pelvic inflammatory disease vs women who have not.

The odds ratio (OR) for ectopic pregnancy comparing women with pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) to those without PID is calculated as:

\[ \text{OR} = \frac{a \cdot d}{b \cdot c}, \]

where:

  • \(a = 28\) (Ectopic & PID),
  • \(b = 251\) (Ectopic & No PID),
  • \(c = 6\) (No Ectopic & PID),
  • \(d = 273\) (No Ectopic & No PID).

Substituting the values:

\[ \text{OR} = \frac{28 \cdot 273}{251 \cdot 6} = \frac{7644}{1506} \approx 5.07. \]

c. Find a 99% confidence interval for the population odds ratio.
17(c) 99% Confidence Interval for the Population Odds Ratio

To find the confidence interval (CI) for the population odds ratio (OR), we use the log transformation:

  1. Log of the OR: \[ \ln(\text{OR}) = \ln\left(\frac{a \cdot d}{b \cdot c}\right) \]

  2. Standard Error (SE): \[ SE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d}} \]

  3. 99% CI for log(OR): \[ \ln(\text{OR}) \pm z_{0.005} \cdot SE \] where \[z_{0.005} = 2.576\] for a 99% confidence interval.

  4. Exponentiate the bounds to obtain the CI for OR.

Step-by-Step Calculation

Given: \[a = 28, \, b = 251, \, c = 6, \, d = 273, \, \text{OR} = 5.07.\]

  • Log(OR): \[ \ln(\text{OR}) = \ln(5.07) \approx 1.622 \]

  • Standard Error: \[ SE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{28} + \frac{1}{251} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{273}} = \sqrt{0.0357 + 0.0040 + 0.1667 + 0.0037} = \sqrt{0.2101} \approx 0.458 \]

  • Confidence Limits (log scale): \[ 1.622 \pm 2.576 \cdot 0.458 = 1.622 \pm 1.18 \] which gives: \[ [0.442, \, 2.802] \]

  • Confidence Limits (OR scale): \[ [ e^{0.442}, \, e^{2.802} ] \approx [1.55, \, 16.47] \]

Interpretation: We are 99% confident that the population odds ratio lies between 1.55 and 16.47.

d. Assuming the women were chosen to have equal numbers who had and had not experienced an ectopic pregnancy, what kind of study is this? Is the relative risk that you can calculate from this table meaningful?

Since the study deliberately selected equal numbers of women with ectopic pregnancy (cases) and women without ectopic pregnancy (controls), this is a case-control study.

In a case-control study:
  • The number of cases and controls is fixed by design, not by the natural frequency of the disease in the population.
  • The marginal totals of the table (i.e., the row sums) do not represent population prevalence or risk.

Why Relative Risk is Not Meaningful Here

The relative risk (RR) is defined as: \[ RR = \frac{P(\text{disease} \mid \text{exposed})}{P(\text{disease} \mid \text{not exposed})}. \]

To compute this, we need the true probabilities of disease in exposed and unexposed groups, which requires the sample to reflect population proportions.

However, in a case-control design:
  • The sampling artificially forces the number of cases (279) and non-cases (279) to be equal.
  • This means \(P(\text{disease})\) in this sample is not the true population probability.
As a result, RR calculated from this table would be biased and meaningless because:
  1. The probability of disease is overrepresented (50% in the table vs. a much smaller rate in the real population).
  2. The exposure distribution among controls might not reflect that in the overall population.

What Can We Use Instead?

The odds ratio (OR) remains valid because: - OR is a ratio of odds, which is invariant to the way we sample cases and controls. - When the disease is rare, \(OR \approx RR\), so OR provides a good approximation of RR in the population.

In Conclusion:
This is a case-control study, and RR cannot be meaningfully calculated from this data. The OR is the appropriate measure of association.
